How Wrong Is Your MMM?

The research behind how using a budget phasing algorithm can dramatically tighten the confidence in your MMM results.

The research behind how_wrong_is_your_mmm, the open-source package we built to measure and fix a hidden flaw in MMM elasticity estimates: the numbers usually swing wildly between refits, not because the model is wrong, but because the data was never designed to pin them down.

Section 1

The Problem

Every MMM produces numbers: TV elasticity 0.42, Meta elasticity 0.28, Search elasticity 0.51. These numbers feed into budget recommendations, agency briefs, and board decks. The question most practitioners don't ask often enough is: how much should we trust them?

For most marketing datasets, the honest answer is less than you think, not because the model is broken, but because the data was never designed to answer the question.

Variance, not bias

This is the key technical point, and it matters for how you think about the problem. OLS (ordinary least squares, the standard regression method behind most MMMs) is still unbiased with correlated predictors. Given enough independent data, the average estimate would converge on the truth. The problem is that you don't have enough independent data, and you never will: 2–5 years of weekly observations, trying to identify 10–20 parameters in a world where channels have barely moved independently.

What you get instead is high variance: estimates that are centred near the truth on average, but swing widely in practice. Your specific TV elasticity (the number in your budget model) is one draw from a wide distribution. A company in the same market, with the same true elasticities, but a slightly different observation window, would get a different number.

Below: three channels, known true elasticities (TV 0.30, Meta 0.50, Search 0.40), mean pairwise correlation (the average correlation across every pair of channels) of 0.70. We simulate 50 plausible histories of the same market (same truth, different noise draws) and fit OLS to each one.

The coefficient of variation (standard deviation divided by mean estimate) measures how unstable the estimates are relative to their magnitude. In the simulation above (mean pairwise correlation 0.70), TV elasticity has a CV of 36%. That means if you refitted the same model on a slightly different data window, your number could shift by a third in either direction, not because the market changed, but because the data was never informative enough to pin it down.

The coefficient of variation, and how it scales with correlation

CV is a simple ratio: standard deviation of the estimated elasticity divided by its mean. A CV of 10% means estimates cluster tightly around their average: the model gives consistent answers across different data realisations. A CV of 40% means estimates swing wildly; the answer your specific dataset produced could easily be half or double the truth.

CV is more useful here than a raw standard error, because it's relative to the estimate's own magnitude: a standard error of 0.05 means something very different at an elasticity of 0.10 versus 0.50, while CV normalises for that, making channels and models directly comparable.

Our simulations show this relationship between channel correlation and CV is not linear, it accelerates. Moving from correlation 0.3 to 0.5 roughly doubles TV elasticity CV. Moving from 0.5 to 0.9 more than doubles it again. At 0.9, the CV exceeds 50% for every channel, the model is producing more noise than signal.

Getting a personalised number

Generic thresholds are useful benchmarks, but the most informative diagnostic uses your own spend data (your actual channel correlation structure) rather than a synthetic example.

The approach sidesteps the obvious problem: you don't know the true elasticities. It assigns plausible ones, simulates what sales would look like if they were true, then measures how consistently OLS recovers them from your actual spend data. The collinearity driving the unreliability comes entirely from your real spend history: the sales simulation is just scaffolding to measure it.

The output is an elasticity interval per channel: not a point estimate, but a range. An elasticity interval is honest, but it isn't what a budget conversation runs on, so supply your planned spend per channel and the same simulated distribution rescales into an incremental-revenue range: each draw is multiplied by the planned spend for that channel, then the 10th–90th percentile is reported. That's the number a CMO actually reads.

A narrow range means the channels varied independently enough for the model to distinguish their effects. A wide range (especially one spanning more than the estimate itself) means the data design has failed: the number in your budget model is one of many equally plausible answers. Search typically shows the widest range because it correlates most tightly with overall business activity. This assumes sales is already a £ value; for volume targets, a per-unit value parameter converts to CAC and ROI instead (see the notebooks).

Reading the output: if the range is narrower than 50% of the point estimate, the model is reasonably informative. Wider than the estimate itself, treat it as directional at best. A very wide Search range especially is a rough order-of-magnitude indicator, not a number to optimise against. Run the diagnostic quarterly: widening ranges mean your spend has become more correlated over time, narrowing means the data design is improving.

Section 2

The Cause

Marketing budgets are set by people, not algorithms. The same annual planning cycle controls spending across every channel simultaneously: when the demand forecast rises, TV, Meta and Search budgets all go up together. When a campaign launches, all channels activate at once. When there's a trading push, everyone gets funded.

The result is that week-to-week, channel spend patterns are highly correlated. TV is high when Meta is high. Meta is high when Search is high. They move together because the same business decisions drive them all.

TV Meta Search

TV, Meta and Search spend move together nearly every week in this example plan, an average correlation of 0.68. When business conditions drive one channel up, they all go up, because the same planning cycle and demand signals control all of them.

This is not a modelling problem, it's a data design problem. Real marketing data is generated by business decisions, not experiments. And business decisions, by nature, are coordinated.

What this does to your MMM

An MMM estimates each channel's contribution by asking: "holding everything else constant, what does an extra pound of TV spend do to sales?" But if TV is always high when Meta is also high, the model never observes TV-high with Meta-low. It can't hold everything else constant, because nothing ever varies independently.

The result is that the model has to split the credit between channels that always moved together, and it does so inconsistently. Which channel gets the credit can change a lot depending on small, essentially random differences in the data you happened to use.

Collinearity doesn't make the average estimate wrong. It makes any given estimate unreliable, sensitive to which period of data you used, which quarter you refitted, and what noise happened to turn up in your training window.

Why this matters more than you might think

Practitioners see this every time they refit. TV elasticity was 0.42 last quarter; now it's 0.31. The market hasn't changed. The model hasn't changed. Three more months of correlated data shifted the answer by 26%. That's high-variance estimation in practice, not a sign of a broken model, but a sign that the data never gave the model enough to work with.

Most teams respond by investigating the model: different priors, different transformations, different baseline specifications. This rarely helps, because the problem isn't the model. The problem is the data design. Channels that always moved together can't be reliably distinguished by any estimation method, however sophisticated.

The question isn't whether your MMM is biased. It probably isn't. The question is whether the data you gave it contained enough independent variation to pin down the channel effects, or whether the number it produced is just one of many equally plausible answers.

Section 3

The Fix

The diagnostic tells you how unreliable your elasticities are. The fix reduces that unreliability, and it doesn't require changing your annual budget, your channel mix, or your campaign strategy.

The idea is simple: vary the weekly split between channels independently, while keeping monthly totals exactly the same. If TV is heavier in week one, let Meta be lighter, and vice versa the following week. Over time, the channels stop moving in lockstep. The MMM starts to see TV-high / Meta-low periods alongside TV-low / Meta-high periods, and can begin to distinguish their effects.

The mechanism

Within each calendar month, redistribute each channel's weekly spend randomly. One month, TV gets more budget in the first two weeks; the next month, the last two. Meta does the same independently. At the end of each month, rescale so the channel's monthly total is exactly preserved.

Two steps run every month, for every channel: a nudge, then a rescale. Here's exactly what that looks like, with the real numbers from this page's worked example.

Step 1, nudge: each week gets a random adjustment, up or down by a set percentage, or all the way down to £0 on a dark week. Say a channel plans to spend £100k a week for four weeks, £400k for the month, and the algorithm lands on £100k, £120k, £0 and £100k, £320k in total.

Step 2, rescale: that £320k is £80k short of the original £400k. Multiply every week by 400 ÷ 320 (1.25) and you get £125k, £150k, £0 and £125k, which adds back up to exactly £400k.

rawweek = planweek × nudgeweek

phasedweek = rawweek × ( Σplanmonth ÷ Σrawmonth )

That arithmetic is the easy part. The harder part happens before any of this runs: the algorithm searches the nudge size, from small to the maximum you're willing to tolerate, refitting the diagnostic against thousands of simulated alternative histories at each setting, and keeps whichever amplitude narrows your least reliable channel the most (more on how that search works below). Blackout mode runs the identical nudge-then-rescale arithmetic, just with the nudge itself constrained to either 1.0 (full planned spend) or 0.0 (dark week), never anything in between.

The media agency sees the same monthly booking. The annual budget is untouched. The only thing that changes is the within-month timing, which the business already adjusts informally for flights and promotional peaks. What's new is making that adjustment deliberately, independently, and consistently across all channels.

How much variation to introduce, and how we pick it

The amplitude of the phasing (how aggressively the weekly split is reshuffled) is the main dial. We choose it by grid search: testing a default of 20 amplitude levels between none and the maximum allowed, measuring the resulting CV at each, and recommending whichever minimises the worst-case CV across channels, not the average, so no single channel is left unreliable. More variation de-correlates channels faster and produces greater CV reduction; less variation is operationally easier but takes longer to show up in the model.

Our research shows this relationship is roughly linear: each additional 10 percentage points of weekly deviation produces approximately the same incremental improvement. There's no cliff edge or discontinuity, just a smooth trade-off between aggressiveness and benefit.

More weekly variation → greater CV reduction
Max elasticity band-width reduction (%) vs max weekly deviation from original plan · 52-week phased plan

At ±40% weekly deviation (a moderate level that most agencies can accommodate), phasing reduces maximum elasticity uncertainty by ~30% after one year (27–35% across 5 independent phasing draws). At ±80%, the reduction reaches ~53% (49–60% across the same 5 draws). The payoff is roughly linear, there is no magic threshold, just more de-correlation for more variation. The range reflects that "phasing" means one specific random reshuffle of the week-to-week mix; a different draw of the same amplitude gives a slightly different result, which is itself worth remembering before treating any single reduction number as exact.

What you get

The output of the phasing recommendation is a concrete 52-week spend schedule: one number per channel per week, with monthly totals guaranteed to match the original plan exactly. This is what you hand to the media agency, not a strategy document or a framework, but a booking grid.

In this page's real example dataset (4 years of history, 1 year of plan, correlation 0.68), a ±37% weekly deviation on the standard continuous range produces a 16% reduction in maximum CV after one year of phasing (8–21% across 10 independent phasing draws). That's modest because four years of correlated history dilutes one year of de-correlated plan data, and because a continuous range is the gentler of this package's two levers. Blackout does substantially better, covered next.

Channel-specific constraints

Every result so far assumed every channel could move by the same amount. Real media plans rarely work that way: TV is often booked and contracted well ahead of time and simply can't be reshuffled week to week, while a channel like Search or paid social can flex much more freely. The package supports this directly: the weekly deviation allowance can be set per channel, a single number for a symmetric range, or, for a channel that genuinely can be turned off, Blackout: a setting that switches it fully on or fully off rather than nudging it up and down.

Locking a channel costs more than it looks like. If TV can't move at all, phasing still helps, Meta and Search de-correlate from each other and, to a lesser extent, from the now-fixed TV, but most of TV's own reliability gain disappears. In a worked example (three channels, correlation 0.7, ±40% deviation where allowed): phasing all three channels normally reduces worst-case CV by 16%; locking TV and phasing only Meta and Search reduces it by 12%, with TV's own CV barely moving. A channel only gets more reliable if it's actually allowed to vary.

Sometimes the honest constraint from an agency isn't "vary it a bit", it's "it's either running or it's off." Blackout models that directly: instead of a continuous ± range, each week is either spent at the full planned amount or at exactly zero, nothing in between. Left uncapped, a bad month could land several dark weeks at once, forcing the few surviving weeks to carry an implausible multiple of the original budget, so a cap bounds how many dark weeks a month are allowed: one, by default, so at most one week's spend ever needs a new home.

Because it concentrates variation into a genuine on/off switch rather than spreading it thinly, Blackout buys more reliability per unit of disruption than a continuous range at the same nominal deviation budget. Checked across three independent data draws (same 3-channel, correlation-0.7 scenario used throughout this guide):

Section 4

The Impact

Section 3 showed what to do. This section answers how big the benefit is, how long it takes, and whether it holds up at realistic channel counts.

The short answer: one year of phasing against four years of correlated history produces a modest improvement (roughly 16–30% CV reduction, depending on deviation amplitude, with some spread depending on which specific de-correlated schedule you happen to run). The benefit is real, but it takes time to compound. As phased periods accumulate, they displace correlated history and the model becomes progressively more reliable.

Time and aggressiveness are substitutes. ±80% deviation reaches ~53% CV reduction in one year. ±40% deviation needs roughly 2.5 years to reach the same level. Does it scale?

Every result so far used a three-channel toy example: TV, Meta, Search. Real MMMs usually run more, paid social, paid search, display, OOH, radio, affiliates, and often more besides. Before trusting any of this on a real spend history, it's worth asking the obvious question: does the story hold up once you're past three channels?

We reran the diagnostic and the phasing recommendation at channel counts from 5 to 20, generic channels with randomly drawn elasticities (not hand-picked, to avoid stacking the deck), same demand-driven collinearity structure used throughout this guide.

All channels in this simulation share one latent demand signal, so more channels competing for that same signal could plausibly make individual effects harder to pin down. Holding correlation fixed at 0.7, here's what actually happens as channel count grows:

Section 6

Questions

Doesn't this need me to already know my elasticity?

You supply a plausible elasticity, not a proven one. Section 1 has the full mechanics. The diagnostic simulates sales as if that number were true, then checks how reliably OLS recovers it from your actual spend pattern. The unreliability it finds comes from your spend history, not your choice of elasticity, and that's a checkable claim, not an assertion: rerun the diagnostic with a meaningfully different elasticity and the CV barely moves, because it's the channels' correlation doing the work, not the number you picked.

How do I phase a plan I haven't finalised yet?

BudgetPhaser needs a starting weekly shape to work with, so build one the normal way: take your annual per-channel budget from your MMM and optimiser, and spread it across the year using whatever seasonality or demand pattern you'd use anyway. That first pass doesn't need to be right, phasing is about to rework it regardless. Feed it in alongside your spend history, and the output is your real weekly booking plan, decorrelated from day one instead of retrofitted onto something the agency's already committed to.

Why not just run a geo-lift test instead?

Budget phasing isn't A/B testing, and it isn't a geo-experiment either. There's no control group, and no efficiency sacrificed in some markets to learn in others: the spend still goes where it was always going, only the timing varies more. It's a by-product of normal media operations, not a separate experiment running alongside them.

Geo-experiments (holding out matched control markets to measure incremental lift) are a genuine gold standard for causal identification, and where they're feasible, they should usually be preferred to any observational fix. But "just run a geo-test" undersells the practical cost, on four counts. It's impractical to run one for every channel simultaneously, TV, Meta and Search all at once, without the tests confounding each other. Each test produces one incrementality estimate for one channel in one period, not the ongoing, time-varying elasticities a budget allocator needs across the whole plan. Running one means genuinely holding spend back in control markets for the test window, a real, deliberate cost, not a hypothetical one. And it requires media buying and measurement infrastructure most teams don't have set up for continuous use.

Even where a geo-test is run, it isn't the clean fallback it's often presented as. Recast's June 2026 open-source benchmark study ran 32,000 simulated experiments across four widely-used geo-lift tools, CausalPy, Google Matched Markets, Meta GeoLift, and Google Causal Impact, under conditions practitioners actually encounter: an outlier test market, a small donor pool, and a short pre-treatment calibration window.

The headline finding: every tool trades off two failure modes, and none escapes the trade-off. Meta GeoLift keeps its coverage promise and rarely false-alarms, but its intervals are wide enough that most tests simply come back inconclusive. Google Causal Impact sits at the other end: it actually detects real effects, but false-alarms far more often and carries a consistent positive bias. CausalPy and Google Matched Markets land in between, under-covering relative to their nominal 95%. And the conditions that make this worse, a short pre-treatment window, a small donor pool, an unusually large test market, are common, not edge cases.

Meta GeoLift: detects a real 7.5% lift only 4–11% of the time, but false-alarms just 3–5% of the time, wide, cautious intervals that mostly come back inconclusive. Google Causal Impact: detects it 52–66% of the time, but false-alarms nearly 30% of the time. CausalPy and Google Matched Markets land in between on both counts, under-covering (76–86% actual vs. 95% nominal). Under a 30-day rather than 90-day pre-treatment window, CausalPy and Causal Impact's false-positive rates both climb above 24%.

None of this means these tools are broken, they're doing exactly what their designers intended, and each is defensible depending on which mistake is more expensive for your business. The point is narrower: "just geo-test it" swaps one uncertainty problem (channel collinearity in an MMM) for another (calibration trade-offs in a geo-experiment tool), and doesn't scale to covering every channel in the plan. It's a genuinely useful complementary check, not a clean substitute.

Doesn't a Bayesian model already fix this?

A common reaction: "we don't use plain OLS, we use a Bayesian MMM, priors regularise the estimates, so surely this isn't our problem." A prior is a statement about what you believed before seeing the data. In a Bayesian regression, it shrinks noisy estimates toward a plausible range and stabilises models that would otherwise overfit thin or noisy data, that's genuinely useful. But collinearity isn't a noisy-estimation problem in that sense: when TV and Meta spend move almost together, the likelihood is nearly flat along a ridge, dozens of very different (TV effect, Meta effect) pairs explain the data almost equally well, because the data never showed the model TV moving without Meta.

Priors regularise noise. They can't manufacture the independent variation that was never in the data to begin with.

We checked this directly: the highest-collinearity scenario from Section 1 (three channels, correlation 0.9, where OLS elasticity CV hits 60%), fit two ways, classical OLS with an analytic 95% confidence interval, and a Bayesian linear model in PyMC with deliberately weak, untuned priors. Averaged across ten independent noise draws (a single draw can itself be a lucky or unlucky one, the same phenomenon this guide is about, one level down):

Weakly-informative priors narrow the interval a little, not a lot
Interval narrowing vs plain OLS · same data, correlation = 0.9 · averaged over 10 noise draws

Individual draws varied more, per-draw narrowing ranged from around 14% narrower to a slight ~3% widening in a few isolated draws for TV and Meta, a reminder that any single comparison is noisy; the mean across draws is the stable number. A real, modest regularisation effect, not a resolution of the underlying identification problem.

Roughly 5–9% tighter intervals is what weakly-informative priors bought here, not nothing, but nowhere close to fixing a problem that leaves OLS's own interval spanning most of the plausible range. This isn't an argument against Bayesian MMMs generally: tools like Meridian and Robyn earn their keep on a genuinely different problem, where the data does contain information the prior can usefully encode. Channel-level collinearity is a different failure mode entirely.

A strongly informative prior can produce a tight interval, but only if the prior itself is right, and the external knowledge to justify one (category benchmarks, a validated rate card) is exactly what's missing here. A confidently narrow interval built on an uncalibrated prior is worse than an honestly wide one: it looks precise enough to act on, and the budget decision built on it fails silently.

Does this help with adstock and saturation too?

That's a different problem. This package is about cross-channel collinearity: can the model tell TV's contribution from Meta's when their spend moves together. Adstock and saturation are about a single channel's own functional form: how spend today carries over, and how quickly returns diminish as spend rises.

A 2024 paper by Dew, Padilla and Shchetkina, "Your MMM is Broken," found that decay, saturation curvature and time-varying effectiveness are often not identifiable either. Different explanations can produce the same observed pattern from typical autocorrelated spend data. Their fix: spend patterns designed to pin down which explanation is right.

That's the same idea phasing already uses, just aimed at a different symptom. Blackout creates on-off cycling, which reveals decay directly. The continuous lever widens the spend range a channel gets observed at, which is what you need to trace out a saturation curve properly. So there's a real reason to think phasing might help here too.

But it's untested. Nothing in this package's simulations models adstock or saturation, so treat this as a hypothesis, not a finding.

Does it scale to all of my channels?

Yes. Section 4 has the full channel-count sweep: band-width reduction from phasing holds flat at 15–18% from 5 to 20 channels tested, because what drives unreliability is correlation between channels, not how many of them there are.

How long until I see the benefit?

Not a multi-year commitment. Section 4 has the full time-to-benefit curve. In this page's example scenario, one year of Blackout gets the least reliable channel 54% narrower, two years compounds that to 61%. The generic sweep in Section 4 shows the same shape holds across amplitudes and channel counts: the biggest gains land in year one, further years add diminishing but real improvement.

Isn't 12 months a long time to wait for this to pay off?

No. Run the same lever for one quarter on this page's example scenario and Search is already about 40% narrower, roughly three-quarters of the way to the one-year figure. That's faster than the usual cycle of bedding in a new MMM partner for two years, deciding it isn't working, then switching to someone else. This runs on the spend data you already have, so the first real signal lands this quarter.

Won't this put my channels into learning mode?

Possibly, and that's a real short-term trade-off, not a hypothetical one: most paid social and search platforms' delivery algorithms recalibrate when a campaign's budget or pacing changes materially, and a full Blackout week is about as material a change as exists. This isn't a decision the model makes alone, it's a compromise between data science and media buying, and Section 3 covers making it channel by channel: the weekly deviation allowance ranges from 0% (untouched) through a gentle continuous nudge up to Blackout, so a channel that can't tolerate any disruption can simply be locked at 0% while the others still de-correlate around it.

What do I actually hand my media agency?

A concrete 52-week spend schedule, one number per channel per week, covered in full in Section 3. Every month still totals exactly what it does today; nothing about the buy itself changes, only which week the money lands in.

Section 7

Bringing It Together

Marketing channels move together because business decisions are coordinated. That makes elasticity estimates unreliable, not biased: your model's number is one draw from a wide distribution, and the CV measures how wide it is for your own spend history. Budget phasing fixes this directly: vary the weekly channel split within each month, keep monthly totals exactly the same, and it works whether your MMM has three channels or twenty. Blackout buys more reliability per week of disruption than a continuous swing. And switching to a Bayesian model doesn't sidestep any of this: priors regularise noise, they don't manufacture variation the data never had.

The question isn't whether to trust your MMM. It's whether your data gave it a fair chance. Budget phasing is how you give it one.
