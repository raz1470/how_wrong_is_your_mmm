How Wrong Is Your MMM?

An overview of how using a budget phasing algorithm can dramatically tighten the confidence in your MMM results.

1 · The problem 2 · The cause 3 · The fix 4 · The algorithm 5 · The impact 6 · How it works 7 · Questions

Here's an uncomfortable question. Take the marketing mix model (MMM) you use to allocate your marketing budget across channels. Now run it again: same channels, same market, just a slightly different slice of history. Would it give you the same answer?

For most brands, no. Here's why, and what to do about it.

1 · The problem

Your model is more unsure than it lets on

Take an example £12.3m annual plan: £5.1m on TV, £4.1m on Meta, £3.1m on Search. Ask the model for a single number and you'll get one. Ask it to be honest about the range of answers the same data could plausibly support, and the picture changes.

Look closely and none of these ranges are tight. TV could be earning anywhere from £945k to £2.09m off the same spend, more than double. Search's range is almost as wide in absolute terms, on barely a third of the budget. That's not a rounding error. That's the model telling you it genuinely doesn't know which channel is really carrying the plan.

2 · The cause

Your channels never move on their own

Here's why the model is so unsure. TV, Meta and Search budgets get set in the same planning cycle, so when one goes up, they usually all go up. Look at an example planned year of spend below: the three lines rise and fall almost in lockstep.

TV, Meta and Search spend move together nearly every week, an average correlation of 0.68. Your model has never actually seen TV move independently of the other two, so it can't confidently tell you how much of your sales are down to TV rather than Meta or Search.

It isn't that the model is badly built. It's that the data it's learning from was never designed to answer this question.

3 · The fix

Same budget, a smarter phasing algorithm

You don't need a bigger model, a bigger budget, or an AI agent bolted onto your MMM. You need to break the correlation between channels using a phasing algorithm. Nudge a channel's weekly spend up or down by a set percentage, or go further and switch it off entirely some weeks, giving that spend to another week instead. Either way, the month still lands on exactly the same total for each channel. Breaking that cycle of correlated spend gives the model weeks where each channel finally moves on its own, so it can tell which one is actually driving your sales.

TV never goes fully dark here, it just runs up to 40% above or below its usual week, still landing on the same monthly total. Meta and Search use the harder on/off lever: some weeks nothing at all, the weeks that stay on absorbing that spend on top of their own. Same guarantee either way, just applied differently per channel.

4 · The algorithm

What the algorithm actually does

Two steps run every month, for every channel: a nudge, then a rescale. Here's exactly what that looks like, with real numbers, and what's actually driving the choice underneath it.

Step 1, nudge: each week gets a random adjustment, up or down by a set percentage, or all the way down to £0 on a dark week. Say a channel plans to spend £100k a week for four weeks, £400k for the month, and the algorithm lands on £100k, £120k, £0 and £100k, £320k in total.

Step 2, rescale: that £320k is £80k short of the original £400k. Multiply every week by 400 ÷ 320 (1.25) and you get £125k, £150k, £0 and £125k, which adds back up to exactly £400k.

rawweek = planweek × nudgeweek

phasedweek = rawweek × ( Σplanmonth ÷ Σrawmonth )

That arithmetic is the easy part. The harder part happens before any of this runs. For every channel, the algorithm searches the nudge size, from small to the maximum you're willing to tolerate, refitting the model against thousands of simulated alternative histories at each setting, and keeps whichever one narrows your least reliable channel the most.

6 · How it works

One pipeline, three steps

All of this runs through how_wrong_is_your_mmm, a free, open-source python package. Point it at your own spend history and it runs the same three steps on your numbers, not a hypothetical example.

The diagnostic tells you how wide your true range is. Phasing gives you a spend schedule that narrows it, same budget, same totals. Retraining is where the payoff lands.

Run all three steps against your own spend and ReportBuilder packages the result into a single client-ready HTML report: today's honest range, the recommended weekly schedule, and how much it narrows things, no code required to read it. See a full example report →

7 · Questions

Frequently asked questions

Doesn't this need me to already know my elasticity?

You supply a plausible one, not a proven one, that's the whole trick. The model simulates what your sales would look like if that number were true, then checks how reliably it can recover it from your actual spend pattern. The unreliability it finds comes from your channels moving together in real life, not from the number you picked. Pick a different plausible number and the diagnosis barely changes, your spend pattern is doing the work.

Why not just run a geo-lift test instead?

Geo-experiments are the academic gold standard, but they test one channel at a time, need a real blackout region, and take months to read. You can't run one for TV, Meta and Search simultaneously. This works with the spend and channel mix you already have.

Doesn't a Bayesian model already fix this?

Not on its own. Priors are genuinely useful, they stabilise noisy estimates and help capture things like how spend decays or saturates over time. But they can't invent information that was never in the data: if TV and Meta always moved together, no prior tells you which one actually drove sales. We tested it directly, and switching to a Bayesian fit only narrowed the uncertainty by 5 to 9%.

Does this help with adstock and saturation too?

Different problem, and a real one. A 2024 study found decay, saturation and time-varying effectiveness are often impossible to tell apart from ordinary spend data either. Phasing wasn't built to fix that, but it runs on the same idea: deliberately varying spend. That probably helps here too.

Does it scale to all of my channels?

Yes. Everything on this page uses three channels for clarity, but we reran the same diagnostic and phasing fix at 5, 10, 15 and 20 channels. Reliability barely moves as you add more, what matters is how correlated your channels are with each other, not how many you have. Phasing delivers roughly the same improvement the whole way, from 5 channels to 20.

How long until I see the benefit?

This isn't a multi-year commitment. Year one alone gets Search to 54% narrower, not a promise for week one, but not three years away either. Stick with it and it keeps compounding: 61% by year two. You don't need to wait to see a real difference, and it gets better if you do.

Isn't 12 months a long time to wait for this to pay off?

No, it moves faster than that. Run the same on/off lever for just one quarter on this page's own scenario, and Search is already about 40% narrower, roughly three-quarters of the way to the 54% you get after a full year. Compare that to the common cycle: two years bedding in a new MMM partner, deciding it isn't working, then starting over with someone else, without ever building on what you learned. This runs on the model and spend data you already have, so the first real signal lands this quarter, not after another vendor switch.

Won't this put my channels into learning mode?

Possibly, in the short term, and that's the real trade-off: how much tighter do you want your results? A channel's reliability gain compounds over a year or two, usually worth more than what a learning-phase reset costs today. This isn't a call the model makes alone, it's a compromise between data science and marketing. Each channel's setting is flexible, from 0% (untouched) through a gentle nudge up to 80%, or a full on/off switch, so you can dial each one to wherever that compromise lands.

What do I actually hand my media agency?

A weekly spend number per channel, covering the same plan year. Every month still totals exactly what it does today. Nothing about the buy itself changes, only which week the money lands in.
