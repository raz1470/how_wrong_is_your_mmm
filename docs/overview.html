How Wrong Is Your MMM?

An overview of how using a budget phasing algorithm can dramatically narrow the uncertainty in your MMM results.

1 · The problem 2 · The cause 3 · The fix 4 · The algorithm 5 · The impact 6 · How it works 7 · Questions 8 · Bringing it together

Measuring the return on marketing spend is one of the hardest jobs in growth, and Marketing Mix Modelling has spent the last few years coming back into fashion as the answer to it. Open-source releases have driven most of that: Robyn from Meta, Meridian from Google, PyMC-Marketing from PyMC Labs. Running an MMM has never been easier. Trusting one is a different question.

Google set out why back in 2017, in a paper that is still the clearest statement of what goes wrong. Two of the problems it names do most of the damage, and both trace back to the same root cause: your spend follows demand.

  • Multi-collinearity. TV, Meta and Search get set in the same planning cycle, so they rise and fall together. No model can separate channels that never moved apart, so the estimates come back with high variance.
  • Selection bias. Spend follows demand, because that is what good planning looks like, and demand itself is rarely in the model. The channel coefficients absorb it instead, which is textbook omitted variable bias: the estimate is wrong, not just noisy.

The paper's own answer was better data. Nearly a decade on, the industry's main response has been incrementality testing, now increasingly used to calibrate the model rather than replace it. That is real progress, but it reads one channel at a time and takes months.

Step back and both problems have the same textbook solution: randomisation. This page asks whether a budget phasing algorithm can help with multi-collinearity and selection bias, reducing variance and bias without running an experiment at all.

What this page is: a walkthrough of why marketing mix models struggle to tell your channels apart, and a method that fixes it without changing a single monthly budget.

1 · The problem

Your model is more unsure than it lets on

You have probably seen this already. TV's marginal return was 1.8 last quarter and now it's 1.3. The market hasn't changed. The model hasn't changed. Three more months of data moved the answer by 28%.

Take an example £12.3m annual plan: £5.1m on TV, £4.1m on Meta, £3.1m on Search. Ask the model for a single number and you'll get one. Refit it on many plausible versions of the same history instead, and watch how much that number actually moves. The picture changes.

Same spend, same market, same true effect. This is the range the model itself produces once you refit it across many plausible versions of the same history. It isn't an empirical range of outcomes, it's an estimated one.

Look closely at how wide those ranges are. TV's (£1.8m–£3.3m) is 59% as wide as the estimate sitting in the middle of it. The model is saying TV's contribution could be £1.8m, or it could be nearly double that, and it can't tell you which. Meta's (£3.3m–£4.8m) is 38% as wide and Search's (£4.0m–£5.5m) 32%. TV and Meta even run into each other at the £3.3m mark, so on this evidence you can't rule out TV contributing more than Meta. That's not a rounding error. That's the model telling you it doesn't know.

This isn't bias. OLS, the regression behind most MMMs, stays unbiased when your channels are correlated, as long as the model itself is specified correctly: given enough independent data the average estimate would land on the truth. The problem is that you don't have enough independent data and you never will. Two to five years of weekly observations, ten to twenty parameters, and channels that have barely moved on their own in all that time. What you get instead is high variance: a number centred near the truth on average that swings widely in practice. Yours is one draw from that spread.

Most teams respond by investigating the model: different priors, different transformations, a different baseline specification. That rarely helps, because the model isn't the problem. Channels that always moved together can't be told apart by any estimation method, however sophisticated.

2 · The cause

Your channels never move on their own

Here's why the model is so unsure. TV, Meta and Search budgets get set in the same planning cycle, so when one goes up, they usually all go up. Look at an example planned year of spend below: the three lines rise and fall almost in lockstep.

TV, Meta and Search spend move together nearly every week, an average correlation of 0.68. Your model has never actually seen TV move independently of the other two, so it can't confidently tell you how much of your sales are down to TV rather than Meta or Search.

On its own that number doesn't mean much. What matters is what it does to the answer your model gives you back.

What that correlation costs you

The cost is not linear. Up to about 0.4 the estimate is noisy but usable. Past 0.7 it climbs steeply, and by 0.9 the typical swing is about 60% of the estimate itself. This plan sits at 0.68, which is squarely in the middle band: not guesswork, but not something to move budget on without checking.

It isn't that the model is badly built. It's that the data it's learning from was never designed to answer this question.

3 · The fix

Same budget, a smarter phasing algorithm

You don't need a bigger model, a bigger budget, or an AI agent bolted onto your MMM. You need to break the correlation between channels using a phasing algorithm. Nudge a channel's weekly spend up or down by a set percentage, or go further and switch it off entirely some weeks, giving that spend to another week instead. Either way, the month still lands on exactly the same total for each channel. Breaking that cycle of correlated spend gives the model weeks where each channel finally moves on its own, so it can tell which one is actually driving your sales.

This is less of a departure than it sounds. The business already flexes within-month timing for launches and promotional peaks. What's new is doing it deliberately and independently per channel, rather than as a side effect of whatever else was going on that month.

The idea isn't new either. MMM vendors already say it: Recast tell clients to intentionally vary spend so the model becomes identifiable, and go-dark tests have been around for years. What has been missing is the how much. Which channel to move, by how far, and what you get back for it. That is the algorithm, and it is the rest of this page.

4 · The algorithm

What the algorithm actually does

For every channel, these two steps repeat across each month of the plan: a nudge, then a rescale. Here's exactly what that looks like with real numbers, and what's actually driving the choice underneath it.

Step 1, nudge: each week gets a random adjustment, up or down by a set percentage, or all the way down to £0 on a dark week. Say a channel plans to spend £100k a week for four weeks, £400k for the month, and the algorithm lands on £100k, £120k, £0 and £100k, £320k in total.

Step 2, rescale: that £320k is £80k short of the original £400k. Multiply every week by 400 ÷ 320 (1.25) and you get £125k, £150k, £0 and £125k, which adds back up to exactly £400k.

rawweek = planweek × nudgeweek

phasedweek = rawweek × ( Σplanmonth ÷ Σrawmonth )

That arithmetic is the easy part. The harder part happens before any of this runs, and it comes in two stages. First, each channel is swept on its own from a small nudge up to the full on/off switch, to see which lever suits it best. Then the whole plan is searched together across nudge sizes, refitting the model against thousands of simulated alternative histories at each setting, and keeping whichever size narrows your least reliable channel the most.

That search is what the chart below shows: how much reliability each channel actually buys as the lever is turned up, from leaving it alone through to the full on/off switch.

6 · How it works

One pipeline, three steps

All of this runs through how_wrong_is_your_mmm, a free, open-source Python package. Point it at your own spend history and it runs the same three steps on your numbers, not a hypothetical example.

One pipeline, three steps
What the package does when you point it at your own spend history

Retraining is where the payoff lands, but it only gets there because the first two steps have already broken the correlation.

1

Diagnose

You supply your actual weekly spend by channel and a plausible marginal return for each. The package simulates many plausible versions of that history, refits your model on each one, and measures how far the answer moves between them. What comes back is a range per channel, plus a reliability score for the plan as a whole.

2

Phase

It searches for a weekly schedule that breaks the correlation between your channels, testing how hard each one can be pushed before the disruption costs more than the reliability it buys. Every month's total stays exactly where you planned it. You get a week-by-week spend schedule and a setting per channel, anywhere from an untouched 0% to a full on/off switch.

3

Retrain

You run that schedule, then refit your MMM on the data it produces. Because the channels no longer move together, the model can finally tell them apart, and the ranges come back narrower. Same budget, same annual total, a sharper answer.

Run all three steps against your own spend and ReportBuilder packages the result into a single client-ready HTML report: today's model-estimated range, the recommended weekly schedule, and how much it narrows things, no code required to read it.

7 · Questions

Frequently asked questions

Doesn't this need me to already know my marginal return?

You supply a plausible estimate, not a proven one. The package uses it as the ground truth to measure against: it simulates revenue from that assumption, refits the model across many plausible versions of your history, and reports how far the answer moves. That spread tells you how reliable your model is, not whether your assumed number was right.

Why not just run a geo-lift test instead?

Geo-experiments are the academic gold standard, but they test one channel at a time, need a real blackout region, and take months to read. You can't run one for TV, Meta and Search simultaneously. The tooling is also less settled than it looks: Recast's head-to-head study of four open-source geo-lift packages found none that combines reliable coverage with real detection power, with Meta's GeoLift missing around 90% of genuine effects and Causal Impact raising false alarms about 30% of the time.

Doesn't a Bayesian model already fix this?

Not on its own. Priors are genuinely useful, and Google's own Bayesian MMM paper shows why: they stabilise noisy estimates, and its flexible functional forms capture how spend decays and saturates over time. But priors can't invent information that was never in the data. That paper says as much itself, noting that the optimal media mix it produces “has a large variance due to the variance of the parameter estimates”. If TV and Meta always moved together, no prior tells you which one actually drove sales. We tested it directly: switching to a Bayesian fit only narrowed the uncertainty by 9 to 14%.

How much narrower the interval gets, per channel

Same data, same channels, fitted both ways. The Bayesian interval is narrower, but only by 9 to 14%. Nowhere near enough to separate channels that overlap by the width of the ranges at the top of this page.

But what about hierarchical models?

Genuinely useful, and worth doing if you can. Google's own geo-level hierarchical paper shows that pooling across regions gives tighter intervals than national data alone. But your planning cycle is national, so TV and Search rise and fall together in every region: more rows, the same correlation inside each one. National TV and OOH are bought without a regional breakout, so any geo split there is an allocation rule rather than a measurement, and the paper is candid about what that costs: estimates "generally deteriorate as more media variables are imputed using the national level data". Small regions are noisy on top of that. It helps, but it can't manufacture variation your plan never had.

Does this help with adstock and saturation too?

Different problem, and a real one. A 2024 study bluntly titled Your MMM is Broken found that nonlinear and time-varying effects are often not separately identifiable from ordinary spend data. Adstock makes it worse, because it makes spend even more autocorrelated than it already was. Phasing wasn't built to fix that, but it runs on the same idea: deliberately varying spend. Whether that same trick helps here too is on the roadmap to investigate.

Does it scale to all of my channels?

Yes. Everything on this page uses three channels for clarity, so we swept channel count and correlation together and re-ran the whole fix in every cell. At a realistic 0.7 correlation the on/off lever buys 50 to 53% whether the plan carries 3 channels or 20. What decides how much you gain is how correlated your channels are with each other, not how many of them there are.

How long until I see the benefit?

This isn't a multi-year commitment, and you don't have to wait a full year for the first real signal: run the on/off lever for a single quarter on this page's own scenario and every channel is already 26 to 29% narrower than it is today, about half of what a full year buys. Year one gets TV to 54% narrower. Stick with it and it keeps compounding to around 65% by year two, where all three channels land within a whisker of each other. You don't need to wait to see a real difference. It only gets better if you do.

Won't this put my channels into learning mode?

Possibly, in the short term, and that's the real trade-off: how much tighter do you want your results? A channel's reliability gain compounds over a year or two, usually worth more than what a learning-phase reset costs today. This isn't a call the model makes alone: it's a compromise between data science and marketing. Each channel's setting is flexible, from 0% (untouched), through a gentle nudge, all the way up to 100%, to a full on/off switch. Dial each one to wherever that compromise lands.

What do I actually hand my media agency?

A weekly spend number per channel, covering the same plan year. Every month still totals exactly what it does today. Nothing about the buy itself changes, only which week the money lands in.

How do I phase a plan I haven't finalised yet?

BudgetPhaser needs a starting weekly shape to work with, so build one the normal way: take your annual per-channel budget from your MMM and optimiser, and spread it across the year using whatever seasonality or demand pattern you'd use anyway. That first pass doesn't need to be right, phasing is about to rework it regardless. Feed it in alongside your spend history, and the output is your real weekly booking plan, decorrelated from day one instead of retrofitted onto something the agency's already committed to.

8 · Bringing it together

It was never the model's fault

Marketing channels move together because business decisions are coordinated. That makes marginal returns hard to identify rather than necessarily wrong: your model's number is one draw from a wide distribution, and this page is a way of measuring how wide. Budget phasing addresses that directly. Vary the weekly split within each month, hold the monthly totals exactly where they are, and it works whether your model has three channels or twenty. The on/off lever buys more reliability per week of disruption than a continuous swing. And a Bayesian model doesn't sidestep any of it: priors regularise noise, they don't manufacture variation the data never had.

The question isn't whether to trust your MMM. It's whether your data gave it a fair chance. Budget phasing is how you give it one.
