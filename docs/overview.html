How Wrong Is Your MMM?

An overview of how a budget phasing algorithm can narrow your MMM's variance, correct its bias and make adstock and saturation identifiable, on the same budget.

1 · The problem 2 · The cause 3 · The fix 4 · The impact 5 · How it works 6 · Questions 7 · Bringing it together

What this page is: a walkthrough of why marketing mix models struggle to tell your channels apart, and a method that fixes it without changing a single monthly budget.

Measuring the return on marketing spend is one of the hardest jobs in growth, and Marketing Mix Modelling has spent the last few years coming back into fashion as the answer to it. Open-source releases have driven most of that: Robyn from Meta, Meridian from Google, PyMC-Marketing from PyMC Labs. Running an MMM has never been easier. Trusting one is a different question.

Google set out why back in 2017, in a paper that is still the clearest statement of what goes wrong. Three of the problems it names, two directly and a third a 2024 follow-up made explicit, do most of the damage. All three trace back to the same root cause: your spend follows demand.

  • Multi-collinearity. Marketing channels get set in the same planning cycle, so they rise and fall together. No model can separate channels that never moved apart, so the estimates come back with high variance.
  • Selection bias. Spend follows demand, because that is common practice, but demand itself isn't directly observable, so the model has to fall back on proxies for it. The channel coefficients absorb what those proxies miss: textbook omitted variable bias, an estimate that is wrong, not just noisy.
  • Non-identifiable adstock and saturation. Every MMM also has to work out how long an ad's effect lingers, and when extra spend stops buying much more. A 2024 study titled Your MMM is Broken found these shape parameters are often not separately identifiable from ordinary spend data either, for the same underlying reason: spend that never varies on its own can't tell the model what shape its response curve takes.

Google's 2017 paper's own answer was better data. Nearly a decade on, the industry's main response has been incrementality testing, now increasingly used to calibrate the model rather than replace it. That is real progress, but it reads one channel at a time and takes months.

Step back and all three problems have the same textbook solution: variation. This page asks whether a budget phasing algorithm can help with all three, narrowing variance, correcting bias and tightening adstock/saturation ranges, without running an experiment at all.

1 · The problem

Your model is more unsure than it lets on, in three separate ways

You have probably seen the first symptom already. TV's marginal return was 1.8 last quarter and now it's 1.3. The market hasn't changed. The model hasn't changed. Three more months of data moved the answer by nearly a third. That's the visible symptom. Underneath it are three separate diagnoses, and a single MMM run tells you about none of them: how wide the true range around that number is (variance), whether the number is centred on the truth at all (bias), and whether the model even knows the shape of your response curves (identifiability of adstock and saturation).

Take an example £17.3m annual plan across four channels: £5.1m on TV, £4.1m on Meta, £4.1m on Search Generic, £4.0m on TikTok. Ask the model for a single number on each and you'll get one. Refit it on many plausible versions of the same history instead, and all three problems show up in the same run.

Look closely at how wide those ranges are, not just where they sit. TV's could be £2.2m or well over double that, and the model can't tell you which. Several channels overlap each other's ranges too: TV and Meta's by more than £2m, and Meta's in turn overlaps both Search Generic's and TikTok's, so three of the four channels can't be ruled apart from at least one neighbour on this evidence. That's not a rounding error. That's the model telling you it doesn't know.

This part isn't bias on its own. OLS, the regression behind most MMMs, stays unbiased when your channels are correlated, as long as the model itself is specified correctly: given enough independent data the average estimate would land on the truth. The problem is that you don't have enough independent data and you never will. Two to five years of weekly observations, ten to twenty parameters, and channels that have barely moved on their own in all that time. What you get instead is high variance: a number centred near the truth on average that swings widely in practice. Yours is one draw from that spread.

TV is systematically overstated: even its low end sits above the true-revenue line. Meta is the outlier the other direction, its range straddling the true-revenue line instead.

This is the second, separate failure: the demand that drives a good planner's decisions is rarely in the model at all, so the channel coefficients absorb it instead. TV is overstated by more than a jump in variance would explain on its own, while Meta's range straddles the true-revenue line instead, because its own true marginal return sits closest to the average across channels and left less demand signal for its coefficient to absorb. That's textbook omitted variable bias, and unlike variance it doesn't average out with more data. Refitting the same mis-specified model on more weeks just gets you a tighter estimate of the wrong number.

The third failure is different again: not how wide the range is, or where it's centred, but whether the model can even tell what shape your response curve takes. Adstock and saturation are both curvature parameters, decay and diminishing returns, and a model estimated from spend that never varied on its own has no way to pin either one down.

Most teams respond to any one of these three by investigating the model: different priors, different transformations, a different baseline or curvature specification. That rarely helps, because the model isn't the problem. Channels that always moved together can't be told apart by any estimation method, however sophisticated, on any of these three questions.

2 · The cause

Your channels never move on their own

Here's why the model is so unsure on all three counts. TV, Meta, Search Generic and TikTok budgets get set in the same planning cycle, so when one goes up, they usually all go up. Look at an example planned year of spend below: all four lines rise and fall almost in lockstep.

All four channels move together nearly every week, a mean pairwise correlation of 0.70. Your model has never actually seen TV move independently of the other three, so it can't confidently tell you how much of your sales are down to TV rather than the rest, whether that estimate is centred correctly, or what shape TV's response curve takes.

The 0.70 mean pairwise correlation above is an average. Read the individual cells and TV/Meta, TV/TikTok and Meta/TikTok are all at 0.70 or above; Search Generic is the closest thing to an exception, and even its weakest pair (with TikTok) is still 0.64.

On its own, a correlation of 0.70 doesn't mean much. What matters is what it does to the answer your model gives you back. The clearest illustration is still variance: hold total spend, the true marginal returns and every channel's response curve fixed, vary only the correlation between channels, and any change in the result is attributable to correlation alone. Bias and identifiability would each need a real demand signal or a real response curve varied in the same sweep, which this one does not supply.

It isn't that the model is badly built. It's that the data it's learning from was never designed to answer any of these three questions.

3 · The fix

Same budget, a smarter phasing algorithm

You don't need a bigger model, a bigger budget, or an AI agent bolted onto your MMM. You need to break the correlation between channels using a phasing algorithm. Nudge a channel's weekly spend up or down by a set percentage, or go further and switch it off entirely some weeks, giving that spend to another week instead. Either way, the month still lands on exactly the same total for each channel. Breaking that cycle of correlated spend gives the model weeks where each channel finally moves on its own, so it can tell which one is actually driving your sales.

This is less of a departure than it sounds. The business already flexes within-month timing for launches and promotional peaks. What's new is doing it deliberately and independently per channel, rather than as a side effect of whatever else was going on that month.

The idea isn't new either. MMM vendors already say it: Recast tell clients to intentionally vary spend so the model becomes identifiable, and go-dark tests have been around for years. What has been missing is the how much. Which channel to move, by how far, and what you get back for it. That is what the rest of this section covers.

"By how far" is a magnitude and a cap. "Which channel" also has a shape: five ways to spread a channel's weekly deviation across a month, all landing on the same monthly total.

The five phasing shapes
One representative month at an ±80% cap  ·  seed 100, the same draw used elsewhere on this page
ShapeHow the week-to-week size is setOne month
Uniform Size drawn at random between 0% and the cap, sign balanced across the month. The default shape, and the gentlest: most weeks land nowhere near the cap.
Annulus Size drawn between half the cap and the cap, so the timid middle is excluded and every week moves by a meaningful amount.
Edge · today's winner Every week sits at the cap exactly. Only the sign varies, balanced across the month. Maximises the variation injected per unit of cap.
Seesaw Every week sits at the cap exactly, sign strictly alternating week to week rather than balanced-but-randomly-ordered. Trades some variance and identifiability for a bias edge over "edge" itself.
Blackout A different mechanism, not a fifth point on the same scale: each week is either fully dark (£0) or left exactly at plan, never in between. At least one week a month always stays on, and the weeks that do absorb the dark week's budget.

Bars show each week's realised deviation from plan after the monthly-total rescale, one representative draw per shape (not hand-picked), on a shared ±100% scale. The black dot on Blackout's row is a dark week, exactly £0, not a small bar rounded down.

Whichever shape a channel uses, every month still spends exactly what it was always going to spend, on exactly the channels it was going to spend it on, and the annual budget is untouched. All that changes is which week the money lands in, and that is enough to give the model weeks where TV moves and Meta doesn't.

What the algorithm actually does

For every channel, these two steps repeat across each month of the plan: a nudge, then a rescale. Here's exactly what that looks like with real numbers, and what's actually driving the choice underneath it.

Step 1, nudge: each week gets a random adjustment, up or down by a set percentage, or all the way down to £0 on a dark week. Say a channel plans to spend £100k a week for four weeks, £400k for the month, and the algorithm lands on £100k, £120k, £0 and £100k, £320k in total.

Step 2, rescale: that £320k is £80k short of the original £400k. Multiply every week by 400 ÷ 320 (1.25) and you get £125k, £150k, £0 and £125k, which adds back up to exactly £400k.

rawweek = planweek × nudgeweek

phasedweek = rawweek × ( Σplanmonth ÷ Σrawmonth )

Two lines of arithmetic, and that is the whole transformation. Before asking what it does to your model, though, there is a simpler question to settle: does it actually break the correlation? That one you can answer without fitting anything, by measuring how much the channels still move together once the schedule is applied. It does: mean pairwise correlation falls from 0.70 to 0.16, shown in full alongside the results in the impact section below.

That arithmetic is the easy part. The harder part happens before any of this runs, and it comes in two stages. First, each channel is swept on its own from a small nudge up to the full on/off switch, to see which lever suits it best. Then the whole plan is searched together across nudge sizes and shapes, refitting the model against thousands of simulated alternative histories at each setting, and keeping whichever one narrows variance, bias and identifiability together, not just one of the three.

That search is what the chart below shows: as the ±80% (edge, balanced) lever is turned up from untouched to full strength, all three problems improve together, not just the one the lever was named for.

What lever is best, today

±80% (edge, balanced) is where this scenario's search lands among the deployable shapes, and it is the number quoted throughout the rest of this page. A wider sweep now exists -- 24 candidates: every intensity at uniform/edge/seesaw, the Redistribute family (round-robin blackout, freed budget moved to a recipient month, edge layer on top) at the same four intensities, the MonthStep family (each whole month stepped up or down in orthogonal patterns) at the same four intensities, plus Blackout at dark=1 through dark=4/prob=1.0, not just the five compared in the cost table below (see notebooks/05_strategy_comparison.ipynb) -- and on pure rigor, Blackout (dark=4, prob=1.0) beats ±80% (edge, balanced) on all three measures at once.

It isn't recommended, though. _pick_winner scores rigor only, never operational feasibility, and dark=4/prob=1.0 forces whichever single week survives each month to carry roughly 3-4x its normal budget to make the monthly total whole -- not something a media buyer would actually schedule, whatever the identifiability gain. That gap between "wins the sweep" and "a client could run this" is real, and the fix is a smarter lever, not a smarter sweep: something with the annual cadence of Blackout's discipline but sized so no single week has to absorb a whole dark run's spend, and built to disturb a channel's normal pacing as little as possible outside its own deliberate move. Not designed yet -- see SCOPE.md.

4 · The impact

What the phasing algorithm buys you, and what it costs

Breaking the correlation is the mechanism. Tighter, less biased, more identifiable estimates are the point. The winning lever on this scenario, ±80% (edge, balanced), dominates every other candidate tested on all three rigor measures at once. It is not, however, free: under a real per-channel saturation curve it also gives up more plan-period revenue than any of the other four candidates tested. Both things are true together, and a client report should say so.

5 · How it works

One pipeline, three steps

All of this runs through how_wrong_is_your_mmm, a free, open-source Python package. Point it at your own spend history and it runs the same three steps on your numbers, not a hypothetical example.

One pipeline, three steps
What the package does when you point it at your own spend history

Retraining is where the payoff lands, but it only gets there because the first two steps have already broken the correlation.

1

Diagnose

You supply your actual weekly spend by channel, a plausible marginal return for each, and, if you have them, plausible per-channel adstock and saturation. The package simulates many plausible versions of that history, refits your model on each one, and measures variance, bias and adstock/saturation identifiability together. What comes back is a range per channel on all three, plus a reliability score for the plan as a whole.

2

Phase

It searches for a weekly schedule that breaks the correlation between your channels, testing how hard each one can be pushed before the disruption costs more than the reliability it buys. Every month's total stays exactly where you planned it. You get a week-by-week spend schedule and a setting per channel, anywhere from an untouched 0% to a full on/off switch.

3

Retrain

You run that schedule, then refit your MMM on the data it produces. Because the channels no longer move together, the model can finally tell them apart, and the ranges come back narrower. Same budget, same annual total, a sharper answer.

Run all three steps against your own spend and DiscoveryReport packages the result into a single client-ready HTML report: today's model-estimated range, bias and identifiability, the recommended weekly schedule, and what it costs as well as what it buys, no code required to read it.

6 · Questions

Frequently asked questions

Doesn't this need me to already know my marginal return?

You supply a plausible estimate, not a proven one. The package uses it as the ground truth to measure against: it simulates revenue from that assumption, refits the model across many plausible versions of your history, and reports how far the answer moves. That spread tells you how reliable your model is, not whether your assumed number was right.

Why not just run a geo-lift test instead?

Geo-experiments are the academic gold standard, but they test one channel at a time, need a real blackout region, and take months to read. You can't run one for TV, Meta, Search Generic and TikTok simultaneously. The tooling is also less settled than it looks: Recast's head-to-head study of four open-source geo-lift packages found none that combines reliable coverage with real detection power, with Meta's GeoLift missing around 90% of genuine effects and Causal Impact raising false alarms about 30% of the time. If a geo-lift slot is available anyway, weigh it against creative testing instead: Nielsen's own research found creative quality contributes as much to a campaign's sales impact as every other factor combined, targeting included, so a limited testing budget usually moves the needle further spent there.

Doesn't a Bayesian model already fix this?

Not on its own. Priors are genuinely useful, and Google's own Bayesian MMM paper shows why: they stabilise noisy estimates, and its flexible functional forms capture how spend decays and saturates over time. But priors can't invent information that was never in the data. That paper says as much itself, noting that the optimal media mix it produces “has a large variance due to the variance of the parameter estimates”. If TV and Meta always moved together, no prior tells you which one actually drove sales.

But what about hierarchical models?

Genuinely useful, and worth doing if you can. Google's own geo-level hierarchical paper shows that pooling across regions gives tighter intervals than national data alone. But your planning cycle is national, so TV and Search rise and fall together in every region: more rows, the same correlation inside each one. National TV and OOH are bought without a regional breakout, so any geo split there is an allocation rule rather than a measurement, and the paper is candid about what that costs: estimates "generally deteriorate as more media variables are imputed using the national level data". Small regions are noisy on top of that. It helps, but it can't manufacture variation your plan never had.

Does it scale to all of my channels?

Yes. Everything on this page uses four channels for clarity, so we swept channel count and correlation together and re-ran the whole fix in every cell. At a realistic 0.7 correlation the on/off lever buys 50 to 53% whether the plan carries 3 channels or 20. What decides how much you gain is how correlated your channels are with each other, not how many of them there are.

How long until I see the benefit?

You don't have to wait a full year for the first real signal, and the winning lever gets you there fastest. This section isolates timing alone (no saturation or adstock in the fit, since those are covered by the identifiability pillar above), sweeping plan length on the same four-channel scenario. A 52-week plan phased at ±80% (edge, balanced) cuts max CV by 63.6% (0.273 → 0.099). The gain keeps building past one year, not saturating: 69.3% by two years, 79.8% by four. Starting correlation changes how much a year of phasing buys, not whether it helps: at a fixed 52-week plan, reduction scales from 33.6% (correlation 0.3) to 81.0% (correlation 0.9), with no crossover at the high end.

Mostly, but with a caveat worth stating plainly. Won't this put my channels into learning mode?

Possibly, in the short term, and that's the real trade-off: how much tighter do you want your results? A channel's reliability gain compounds over a year or two, usually worth more than what a learning-phase reset costs today. This isn't a call the model makes alone: it's a compromise between data science and marketing. Each channel's setting is flexible, from 0% (untouched), through a gentle nudge, all the way up to 100%, to a full on/off switch. Dial each one to wherever that compromise lands.

What do I actually hand my media agency?

A weekly spend number per channel, covering the same plan year. Every month still totals exactly what it does today. Nothing about the buy itself changes, only which week the money lands in.

How do I phase a plan I haven't finalised yet?

BudgetPhaser needs a starting weekly shape to work with, so build one the normal way: take your annual per-channel budget from your MMM and optimiser, and spread it across the year using whatever seasonality or demand pattern you'd use anyway. That first pass doesn't need to be right, phasing is about to rework it regardless. Feed it in alongside your spend history, and the output is your real weekly booking plan, decorrelated from day one instead of retrofitted onto something the agency's already committed to.

7 · Bringing it together

It was never the model's fault

Marketing channels move together because business decisions are coordinated. That makes marginal returns hard to pin down in three separate ways rather than necessarily wrong: your model's number carries more variance than it lets on, it can be biased by the demand it never saw, and it may not even know the shape of your response curves. This page is a way of measuring all three at once. Budget phasing addresses the root cause directly. Vary the weekly split within each month, hold the monthly totals exactly where they are, and it works whether your model has three channels or twenty. The winning lever narrows variance by 58.7%, cuts bias by 50.3 points and tightens identifiability by 54.7% on this scenario, and it does that at a real cost, 2.77% of plan-period revenue, not a free upgrade. A Bayesian model doesn't sidestep any of it: priors regularise noise, they don't manufacture variation the data never had.

The question isn't whether to trust your MMM. It's whether your data gave it a fair chance, on variance, on bias, and on identifiability. Budget phasing is how you give it one, and this page shows what that costs as well as what it buys.
